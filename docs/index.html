Hack the North 2026 · Dryft kernel challenge · Version 1 write-up

neoKernel

An autoresearch harness for the Qwen3 4B decode step: a judge that replicates the leaderboard on real hardware, a guard that makes the score hard to game, and agents that are allowed to change the engine only through both.

Paarth Sharma · solo Sept 19, 2026 v1

What the leaderboard measures

Dryft hands every team the same model, Qwen/Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 in BF16 on one H100, and one rule: make it produce tokens faster without changing what it says. A submission is an engine.py that owns the whole generation loop. It is scored as output tokens per second, including prefill, as a geometric mean over six hidden workloads, and it must pass five gates per workload: every emitted token must be native Qwen's greedy choice on the same prefix (or within 2.0 logits of it), time to first token and time per output token may not exceed 1.10 times native, peak memory stays under 90 percent, and timing spread across five samples stays under 25 percent.

The three public workloads are batch 1 with 512 prompt tokens and 32 outputs, batch 4 with 2,048 and 32, and batch 16 with 512 and 128. Prompts are 512 to 2,048 tokens against 32 to 128 outputs, so prefill is a real share of the clock, and the geometric mean means the batch-1 workload counts as much as the batch-16 one.

The floor

A decode step reads every weight once. The model is 8,044,936,192 bytes of BF16 weights (tied embedding counted once), and an H100 SXM moves about 3.35 TB/s, so no step can take less than about 2.40 ms, whatever the batch size, until something like exact speculative decoding produces more than one token per weight pass. Prefill has a compute floor of its own. Put together, each workload has a ceiling in tokens per second, and everything on this page is measured against it.

The starter engine, native Transformers with a dynamic cache, sits at 14 to 22 percent of that ceiling on the public shapes. The first kept change moves it to 16 to 25 percent. The distance left is the project.

Where the time goes

Native decode costs 17 to 22 ms per step and is nearly flat from batch 1 to batch 16. Against a 2.4 ms weight floor that says the step is overhead-bound, not bandwidth-bound: the GPU is waiting on Python and on thousands of small launches, not on memory. Decode is 96, 76 and 94 percent of total time on the three public shapes. Prefill, by contrast, is already within about two times of its compute floor.

The first change was the obvious one: preallocate the KV cache at exactly prompt plus output length (shapes are fixed within a workload, and each workload gets a warmup of its own shape), bypass the Transformers wrapper, and capture the decode step as one CUDA graph. That removed the Python. It did not remove the launches. The profile of the kept engine on an H100 shows one graph launch per step and 2,292 kernels inside it, about sixty-four per layer: Transformers' general-purpose layer code builds masks, expands the eight KV heads to thirty-two with copies, and casts and reshapes on every step. Inside a graph each of those slivers still costs microseconds of scheduling and its own tail.

The harness

Every published loop of this kind works the same way: an agent edits, a benchmark scores, the change is kept or reverted. The two things that decide whether such a loop produces anything are whether the benchmark is trustworthy and whether the agent can game it. neoKernel spends most of its code on those two.

The judge replicates Dryft's protocol: a fresh engine process per workload, one warmup of the same shape, timed samples with the clock in the parent process, then a teacher-forced replay of the emitted tokens through an untouched native model to apply the 2.0-logit rule at every position. It runs on Modal, correctness on an L4 (correctness does not care which GPU) and throughput on an H100, so a full experiment costs cents and most experiments die before they reach the H100. Every result carries the physics floor, per-kernel timing and the gap between kernels, following the speed-of-light guidance that Chen et al. found roughly doubles what kernel agents achieve.

The guard exists because two 2026 studies watched agents cheat: InferenceBench saw fake output tokens under optimization pressure, and KernelGuard catalogued result replay, timer monkeypatching and evaluator mutation. So the agent's write scope is engine/ only; the judge is a separate process it cannot import; timing is the judge's; prompts are fresh random ids the agent never sees; a static lint refuses time, CUDA events, sys.modules, exec and anything not importable in Dryft's sandbox; and any result faster than the weight floor is flagged as a suspected hack rather than logged as a win.

Calibration, which was a result

Before any engine change, the unchanged starter went through the local judge and through Dryft. Prefill on the two 8,192-token shapes matched Dryft to within one percent, which says the GPU and the timing points are right. Everything dominated by per-step Python was 7 to 11 ms slower per step, and it stayed slower after requesting eight cores, pinning threads, and moving to a binary pipe with under 0.5 ms of overhead. CPU time per step equalled wall time per step: the container's single core is simply slower than Dryft's, and native Transformers decode is single-threaded Python. That residual cannot be fixed, so the judge does what Dryft does and gates every candidate against native measured in the same container, moments earlier, through the same pipe. The host factor shrinks as the engine stops being CPU-bound; it already moved from 0.69 to 0.72 on the smallest shape once graph capture landed.

What the agents did, and did not

The loop's first model was GLM 5.2 on Baseten's API. Given a working baseline and a playbook, it proposed the same item, a static KV cache, four times. The first proposal was a unified diff with inconsistent hunk counts that git apply refused; models are bad at byte-exact diffs, so the harness switched to whole-file replacement. The next three were applied, and two failed the correctness check with tensor-shape errors while one failed the guard on mismatched brackets. Each attempt cost under fifteen cents and under a minute of GPU. None was kept.

The structural rewrite then went to a stronger coding agent, Codex, through the same funnel. Its first version raised throughput on every shape and was rejected by the judge, correctly, on the time-to-first-token gate: prefill had picked up an explicit attention mask, which drops PyTorch's SDPA off the flash path. The second version used the causal path for prefill and a static boolean mask only for decode, passed all six local gates, and became engine #11. On Dryft it scored 288.5 tokens per second against the starter's 217.3.

This is the split InferenceBench measured across fifteen frontier agents: weak at the architectural move, workable on bounded changes to a working base, and beaten by a plain parameter sweep on numeric knobs. neoKernel is built around that finding rather than against it. A strong coding agent does the rewrites that create the switches, a cheap model in a tight loop chooses among the switches, and an LLM-free sweep sets the numbers. All three go through the same judge and the same log, so the record shows who found what. No human wrote engine code. The human built the thing that decides what stays.

What version 1 is, and is not

It is a calibrated evaluator, a judge that has already refused one result correctly, a guard that has caught one malformed proposal, and a log with names on every line. It is one kept optimization on the board. It is not yet a search: the agent has kept nothing on its own, and there was nothing for it to search until the engine's operations became its own. The next change, a hand-rolled decode step over the loaded weights with fused norms, packed projections and grouped-query attention that reads the eight KV heads directly, is what turns the sixty-kernel layer into a dozen and gives the loop switches to flip. Whether the sentence at the top of this page ends up true is decided by what the log says after that.

Spend so far: about $3.70 of Modal credit across 22 judge calls, and a few cents of Baseten credit for the agent's proposals. The Dryft runs were free.
